The one thing that can sink you — say it yourselves, in the demo

Your v0.1.0 device test found that a mispronounced word is still accepted on a real phone. The cause is almost certainly architectural: Android’s Filipino dictation service is a language model, so handed a child’s approximate “ka-ha” it returns the nearest real Filipino word — possibly “kuha”. Your checker receives an already-corrected string and never sees the error. No amount of strictness in your code can recover information the recognizer threw away.

Frame it as a finding, not a bug. It is exactly what Bhardwaj et al. (2022) and Santiago et al. (2025) predict — Santiago’s adult-Filipino model scored 39.70% WER on children’s speech, and their recommendation is to embed a fine-tuned recognizer in an interactive platform. You are that platform, using a stock recognizer, and you found the problem they predict. Stated by you it is research. Found by a panelist it is a hole.

The pronunciation checker, in the order you should explain it

  1. Canonicalize both sides. Strip accents and punctuation → map Spanish-era spellings (qu→k, ll→ly, x→ks, then c→k z→s f→p v→b j→h) → fold e→i and o→u → fold the uw/w glide → collapse doubled letters.
  2. Drop 21 filler words (po, mo, na, ang, ay…) — unless filler is all that was said.
  3. Require an exact match of the whole phrase, any single token, or everything run together.
  4. The fragment guard: a token is refused if the whole utterance run together is some other taught word. “pag kain” is Pagkain badly split, so the “kain” in it does not answer Kain.
  5. The variants table: curated per-word mistranscriptions — “i love” for Ilaw, “lines” for Linis, “oppo” for Upo.
  6. One consonant slip, only on canonical forms of 7+ letters, and only if every vowel still lines up.

Why e/i and o/u fold but a/u does not: e/i and o/u genuinely alternate in Filipino — babae/babai, noon/nuon — and the recognizer returns either spelling. a/u never alternates. That single asymmetry is what makes “kaha” fail for Kuha, and it is the best thirty seconds of technical explanation you own.

A · Background, problem, objectives

1What is SALINLAHI, in one sentence?
A voice-interactive Filipino vocabulary game for Kinder–Grade 3 where the child says a Filipino word out loud and that word is what makes the game move.
2What problem does it solve?
English-dominant Filipino children can recognise Filipino words but cannot produce them. Most apps let a child be right by tapping. We made saying it the only way forward.
3Your theoretical basis?
Swain’s Output Hypothesis (producing language deepens processing) → the mechanic. Krashen’s Input Hypothesis (meaning before use) → the teach line before the ask line. Plus digital game-based language learning.
4How is this different from Ibigkas! or Duolingo?
Ibigkas! is Filipino but drill-based and for Grades 4–6, and voice is not the control. Wizardly and Studycat are voice-controlled but English. Nobody has a Kinder–Grade 3 child speaking a Filipino word that performs the action. SHOWTable 2.1.
5Who exactly are your users?
Children K–Grade 3, English-dominant at home, including pre-readers. Teachers and parents are stakeholders, not users.
6Have real children tested this?
Not yet as a formal evaluation — v0.1.0 was device-tested by the group, which is where we found the pronunciation issue. The pupil evaluation is Objective 5 and belongs to Chapter 4. Do not overclaim. Say the date and the school.
7Why the name?
It means lineage. The vocabulary is the household Filipino that passes from a grandparent to a child in conversation, not from a classroom word list.
8Scope and delimitations?
Android only (forced by the plugin); three environments; a selected word set; vocabulary not grammar; supplementary not a replacement; and explicitly not usable by a child who cannot produce clear speech.

B · Architecture and implementation

9Describe your architecture.
Four parts on the device — presentation, game logic, speech input, local data — plus a Supabase backend. They talk through C# events, so no part reaches into another’s internals.
10Walk me through what happens when a child says “Bangon”.
Recognizer returns text → VoiceCommand.ProcessRecognizedText → PronunciationChecker.IsCorrect → OnAnswerChecked(true) → SceneObjectiveController hides the prompt and invokes the objective’s onCorrect → PlayerMovement.Bangon() → RunScoreCounter counts it → Rachel says “Tama!” → the controller waits for the sit-up to finish before the next word. Name each as a file. This question proves you wrote it.
11Why Unity 6 and C#?
2D sprite work, a mature Android pipeline, and the deciding factor — the speech plugin we needed is a Unity package binding to Android’s native recognizer.
12How do you add a word or a scene?
A word is a list entry, not code: {word, variants, promptRoot, instructionClip, onShown, onCorrect}. Fourteen of fifteen scenes are generated by editor builders, so the workflow is edit the builder, re-run, commit.
13Which design patterns?
Observer throughout. Singleton for VoiceNarrator (one mouth, so two lines never overlap). Static utilities for things that are data or pure functions. Strategy-by-data for objectives.
14Why is WakeUpSequence.cs 1,118 lines?
Scene 1 is the only scene with no builder, and it carries the bespoke opening — the snore, the sit-up, the stretch. Volunteer the refactor: split it into the pose sequence, the audio loop and the scene choreography. A known weakness with a plan reads as judgement; defended, it reads as denial.
15How do scripts communicate?
Five events on VoiceCommand, two on the objective controller, UnityEvents per objective. Nothing polls. OnObjectiveCompleted passes the word rather than letting listeners read the field, because that field is cleared synchronously — a real race we closed.
16What if a result arrives after the object is destroyed?
An isShuttingDown flag at the top of every recognizer callback, and OnDestroy cancels the queued restart. Unity’s == lies about destroyed objects, so a null check is not enough.
17How is the HUD built?
At run time from a UITheme ScriptableObject — because regenerated scenes would lose anything hand-placed, and one change updates fifteen scenes.

C · Voice recognition — the heart of it

18How does the voice recognition work?
We don’t do recognition ourselves — we ask Android’s own service to listen in Filipino and give back text, then judge the text. Building a recognizer was never in scope; judging Filipino correctly was.
19Why not string matching or edit distance?
We did, and it was wrong for Filipino. The words are short and separated by one vowel, so one edit of slack accepts every vowel error — “kaha” passed for Kuha, “kanin” passed for Kain, and both are taught in the same scene. A game that teaches pronunciation cannot reward a wrong vowel.
20Walk me through the canonicalization.
The six folds in Part 2 above. Key detail: digraphs first, so ll is not eaten by the single-letter pass; and the concatenation test uses unfiltered tokens, so the filler list does not eat the “po” that is part of Upo.
21Prove two taught words cannot be confused.
All 53 fold to 53 distinct canonical forms, so exact matching cannot make two ambiguous — by construction. Tools > SALINLAHI > Check Pronunciation Matching re-proves it over every cross pair and refuses any variant equal to another taught word. SHOWRun it live. Best 20 seconds you have.
22Then where is the tolerance?
Three narrow places: the variants table, the fragment handling, and one consonant slip on words of 7+ canonical letters where every vowel still matches. Below 7 letters a single consonant is the word.
23What is the fragment guard?
Before accepting a single token we check whether the whole utterance run together is another taught word. “pag kain” is Pagkain split, not an answer to Kain. Same guard stops “ka nin”/Kunin, “u wi”/Upo, “ti ngin”/Bati.
24How did you build the variants table?
Be honest: the entries are still mostly informed seeds. The pipeline is real — play with logging on, pull the CSV, read rows marked wrong where the child said it right, add those exact strings. Bringing one real CSV converts this from a weakness into a method.
25Does it actually reject a mispronounced word on a phone?
See the box on page 1. In our tests yes, every time; on a device not reliably, and we know why — the dictation service returns the nearest real word, so we are handed an already-corrected string. It is the first known issue in v0.1.0, it is why v0.1.0 is a testing build, and it is what defines the next version. Say this in the demo before they ask.
26Why not train your own model?
Out of scope for one year, and the Filipino children’s corpora are recent and mostly not redistributable. We made the game right and treated recognition as replaceable — VoiceCommand is the only script that knows the plugin exists. Swapping it touches one file.
27What if the child speaks while Rachel is talking?
Listening is suspended for the whole line — on a phone speaker the recognizer hears her perfectly and would score it as the child. A result arriving while suspended is discarded as our own Cancel().

D · Gameplay, UX, pedagogy

28Why no penalty or timer?
The user is four to eight and speaking a second language; timing them punishes exactly the behaviour we want. Grounded too: Zargham et al. (2022) found a voice game acting on its own makes things worse when it guesses wrong.
29How are stars and points calculated?
10 points per word plus up to 50 for accuracy. Stars on accuracy only: 3 at ≥95%, 2 at ≥70%, else 1. The best run per scene is kept, never the last.
30How does a non-reader play this?
Every instruction is a voice line plus a picture; on-screen text is a label for the adult. Then concede: twelve words are currently taught by caption only, and that matters precisely because the users are pre-readers. It is on the fix list.
31What happens after repeated failures?
A ladder. Two misses → Rachel says it again slowly, unasked — the most important accessibility behaviour, because a child who cannot reach the replay button still gets help. Four → she points at the button. Ten seconds of silence → encouragement; then a re-prompt every twenty.
32How do you hold attention across 72 objectives?
The vocabulary is a day, not a list — when the game asks for Sipilyo the child is standing at a sink holding one. Five words a scene, and 19 objectives re-ask an earlier word in a different room, so the child retrieves it from the word rather than the picture.
33Doesn’t repetition get boring?
VocabularyMemory: full explanation the first time, a one-line reminder after, within one sitting. Cleared on close, deliberately — a child returning days later should be taught properly, not reminded.
34Why is the narration in English?
The learners are English-dominant — that is the premise. The instruction is in the language they have; the word they must produce is Filipino. And the Filipino word clips were recorded by a group member, not generated, because Tagalog distinguishes by stress — tayô vs táyo — and TTS cannot know which is meant.
35What are the accent marks on some words?
Six of the 53 are minimal pairs the plain spelling cannot disambiguate. Those six carry marks on screen only — nothing reaches the matcher. Marking a word that doesn’t need it teaches a distinction that isn’t there.

E · Data and database

36What is stored, and where?
Two things, two places, on purpose. The learning record — which words, which scenes, how many stars — stays on the phone. Four values go online: id, nickname, total points, total stars.
37Show me the save and retrieve code.
SupabaseClient.SaveScore() POSTs, FetchRecentScores() GETs, SessionScoreTracker decides when. We omit player_id and created_at so the database fills them — and the anon role has no grant on those columns, so a client could not choose its own id or backdate a row.
38Why Supabase?
Managed PostgreSQL with REST, Row Level Security, and Edge Functions — a real server-side check without running a server. And it works from Unity with no SDK.
39The API key ships inside the APK. How is that secure?
The key is public by design; what protects the data is what it is allowed to do. Three layers: Row Level Security (which rows), column grants (which columns — select=* is now refused by the database), and CHECK constraints (what a value may be). No update and no delete, by policy and by grant.
40What stops a child cheating the leaderboard?
The client cannot write it at all — it posts to an Edge Function holding the only key that may write. First submission registers the player and the server issues a 32-byte token; only its SHA-256 is stored; every later submission presents it, compared in constant time. Then concede first: the totals are still the client’s word — no server can replay a child speaking. What it does is refuse the absurd and bound what a forgery is worth.
41Your data privacy position?
Minimisation. No email, no birthdate, no account, no login. A nickname the child chooses, capped at 16 characters. Deleting a profile deliberately leaves the leaderboard row alone. Gap to admit: no parental deletion path yet.
42What if the phone is offline?
Saving never blocks — the run is written to the device before the network is touched, and the leaderboard retries next time the menu opens. But concede the bigger one: the child cannot play at all offline, because recognition itself is a network service.

F · Testing, evaluation, methodology

43How did you test this?
Four layers: compile verification outside Unity with warnings-as-errors; an editor self-check that re-proves no two taught words can collide; nine functional checks against the live backend, two of which must be refused; and device testing. Concede: there is no NUnit suite, and that is the gap.
44Your development methodology?
Iterative and incremental with version control as the backbone — 351 commits, feature branches to main, and a documented release process producing a numbered artifact tied to a commit. Say what you did, not a textbook name.
45Why no pre-test / post-test?
A learning-gain design needs a control group, a longer intervention and a validated instrument. Claiming a gain we cannot support is worse than not claiming one — Muñoz et al. (2025) found a well-received game with no significant gain. Note: Chapter 2’s IPO model still lists a post-test. Fix that before the defense.
46Your performance baseline?
Measured, not estimated: board fetch 226.6 ms median, of which 128.7 ms is connection setup; judgement 5.64 µs and 682 bytes per attempt. All re-runnable.
47Biggest technical challenges?
The matcher accepting wrong vowels (forced the rewrite). The recognizer hearing Rachel through the speaker (forced suspend/resume). The recognizer returning the nearest real word (still open). Bonus: the newer Supabase keys aren’t JWTs, so sending one as a bearer 401’d everything.

G · Limitations and future work

48What are your limitations?
Lead with the device recognizer. Then: Android only; nothing offline; one of sixteen scenes unbuilt; fifteen of the most-heard lines unrecorded; twelve words caption-only; no mini game; and unusable by a child who cannot produce clear speech.
49What would six more months buy?
In order: a children’s-speech recognizer (what Santiago recommends, and the architecture is ready for it), the remaining recordings, Bahay scene 6, offline recognition, a mini game, and a proper pupil evaluation.
50Is this really 60% complete?
By our stated criteria it is 82%, weighted across eight dimensions, and I can recompute it in front of you — the script reads the repository rather than our memory. The weighting is a judgement; the arithmetic is not. Offer the criteria for challenge.
51Why use this instead of a teacher?
It shouldn’t be, and we never claim it should. One teacher with forty pupils gives each child a few chances to say a word out loud. This gives one child a hundred chances in ten minutes, with nobody listening and nothing at stake. It is practice between lessons.
52What is the single weakest part of your system?
Pronunciation correction on a real device. Everything around it works and can be shown; the judgement itself is sound in our tests and unreliable in the field, and we know the mechanism. Answer this honestly — a rehearsed non-answer is worse than the weakness.

Part 4 · What to defend, and how to phrase it

Design decisions that are strengths — claim them, do not apologise for them

Looks likeIs actually
No score penaltyA pedagogical choice grounded in Zargham (2022) and Getman (2023). Punishing a wrong attempt punishes the behaviour we want.
Exact matchingThe stricter and more accurate option. Fuzzy matching is what let “kaha” pass for Kuha.
Android onlyForced by the mechanic — the plugin has no desktop implementation. A technical constraint, not a preference.
MINI GAME does nothingDeliberate. “A button that loads a stub screen would be a worse lie than one that sits still.”
Only 4 values uploadedPrivacy by design. A child’s learning record never leaves the phone.
Captions for pre-readersFor the supervising adult, for a muted phone, and for lines not yet recorded — and they can be switched off per mode.
English narrationThe learners are English-dominant. The Filipino word clips are human-recorded, because stress matters.
Evaluation without learning gainScientific restraint, argued from Muñoz et al. (2025), not an easy way out.

Weaknesses — concede fast, frame once, move on

  • Device pronunciation. “That is our first known issue, and here is the mechanism.” Then the Santiago citation. Never apologise twice.
  • No children have tested it yet. Give the date and the school. Do not improvise a number.
  • No unit test suite. “Our self-check proves the matching property and both assemblies compile with warnings-as-errors — but a proper suite is the gap.”
  • Variants are mostly seeded. “The pipeline is real and the next playtest feeds it.”
  • Documents disagree with the build. “True when written; the count comes from a script now.”
  • One Supabase project for test and release. “No staging database — a test play writes a real row. Known, and the fix is a second project.”
  • 1,118-line script. Volunteer the refactor before they ask for it.

Phrases that work

“I don’t have that number in front of me — it’s in [the file], and I can pull it up.” Never guess a number.
“Yes — that’s in our known issues, and here’s why.” The best possible moment for a weakness they found.
“We tried that first. Here is what went wrong, and here is what replaced it.” Turns a criticism into evidence of iteration.
“That’s a judgement, and here are the criteria — disagree with the weights and I’ll recompute.” Invites challenge instead of defending.

Phrases that lose marks

“It should work.” · “It works on my machine.” · “We ran out of time.” · “That’s just a minor bug.” · “I’m not sure, maybe around fifty-something?” · “That’s the other member’s part.” · Silence while three people look at each other.
